## 1. Is the feedback fair?

**Half fair.** Sakhi already has tools that ask her to do something:
- the 21-day small-steps journeys for PMOS and for iron (walks, protein at breakfast, whole-grain swaps, iron foods);
- the iron tablet tracker;
- the 3-month pelvic floor course;
- Feel better now, with 3-minute routines for cramps, nausea, sleep, mood and hot flushes;
- the pregnancy logs (weight, BP, sugar), the after-birth recovery and mood checks, and the midlife symptom diary;
- petals for doing kind things for herself.

What's missing is what the feedback describes:
1. **The tools are short or scattered.** A 21-day journey ends just as a habit starts forming. Habits take a median of about
   66 days, with a range of 18 to 254 (Lally 2010).
2. **She never sees whether it's working.** No screen says "your period pain is lower than when you started" or "your cycles
   are getting more regular". Progress is what keeps people going.
3. **Movement and food aren't routines.** The PMOS journey says "walk 15 minutes" but gives no plan for the week. There's no
   guided home workout, no weekly plate, and no tracker for active minutes or strength days.
4. **They're hard to find.** They sit as Care tiles among about 80 others. "For you" doesn't surface them from her diary
   (see `docs/for-you-audit-2026-10-23.md`).

**The Indian reality the design has to fit:**
- About half or more of Indian women aren't active enough: 52.4% in the ICMR-WHO NCD survey, 57% in a 2024 Lancet Global
  Health estimate.
- The PCOS guideline names the barriers to plan for: safety outdoors, time, body image, family, and cost.
- She often cooks one meal for the whole family and doesn't choose the menu. The guideline asks to "consider the value of
  broader family engagement".
- Health and fitness apps keep only about 3 to 4% of users by day 30. That's an industry figure, not a study, but the
  direction is clear: a plan has to fit into about 2 minutes a day.

## 3. Advice: turn the scattered tools into 12-week "Plans"

Build one plan engine, then fill it with condition plans one at a time. It should reuse what Sakhi already has: journey steps,
reminders (Version 34), petals, Feel better now's timer and read-aloud, the monthly check-in, the doctor report, and "Ask Qura
to call me".

**What every plan has (from the PCOS guideline's behaviour strategies):**
1. **She picks her goal** (co-developed, rec 3.1.4). Examples: "Less pain next period", "More regular cycles", "More energy",
   "Strong after my baby". She can choose not to focus on weight.
2. **Two or three small daily habits,** each one tap to mark done. A habit grows each week. For example, a walk goes 10, then
   15, 20 and 30 minutes. Missed days never count against her.
3. **A routine she can do at home** with no equipment, 10 to 20 minutes long:
   - a timer, simple drawings, and the steps read aloud in Hindi or English;
   - no video, so the APK stays small and it works offline.
4. **A weekly look-back** on Sunday: what worked, what got in the way, and one fix for next week (problem solving, relapse
   prevention).
5. **Her own progress, from her own diary,** compared with when she started: her period pain, her cycle lengths, her PMS days,
   her hot flushes, her active minutes, and, only if she chooses, her waist size.
6. **When to see a doctor:** if nothing improves after the plan, or a warning sign appears. One tap goes to "Ask Qura to call me".
   The plan's progress can go into the doctor summary if she ticks it.
7. **After 12 weeks:** keep the two habits that helped most, start a maintenance plan, or ask Qura for a plan made for her.
   The guideline says structured, ongoing clinical support works best (rec 3.1.8). That's where Qura's PMOS Program fits, as an
   option. The free plan must stay complete on its own.

**Plans, in the order I'd build them:**

**Trackers to add.** All are on her phone only, and each is one tap:
- **Active minutes today:** 10, 20 or 30, or "did my routine". Later, an optional phone step counter: Android's own step
  sensor, which needs the activity-recognition permission. Not Google Fit, and nothing leaves the phone.
- **Strength day:** yes or no.
- **Plate check:** "Half my plate was vegetables today", yes or no. No calorie counting.

- **Already built, to be pulled into plans:** pain per period, PMS symptoms, hot flushes, iron tablets, pregnancy weight, BP and
  sugar, pelvic floor sessions, and check-in signs.

## 4. Guardrails (what not to do)

- **No calorie counting, no weight-loss targets by default, and no "ideal weight".** The PCOS guideline asks for no unduly
  restrictive diets, weight-inclusive options, and attention to disordered eating during weight management (3.3.3, 3.6.4,
  2.5.1). Counting calories in mixed home food is impractical anyway.
  - The research on tracking is mixed. A survey of 493 students (Simpson and Mazzeo 2017) linked calorie tracking with more
    anxious, restrictive eating, and fitness tracking with eating-disorder symptoms. A 2021 trial of a calorie-counting app
    found no harm.
  - So active minutes are framed as health ("energy, cycles, pain"), never as calories burned or appearance.
  - Add one gentle line to every food screen: if food or weight worries take over her day, talk to a doctor (Tele-MANAS 14416).
- **No supplements, herbs or doses.** Inositol isn't recommended, and ICMR says no protein supplements. Nothing replaces her
  doctor's medicine. The existing journey already follows this rule.
- **No "reverse PCOS" or "cure" claims, in the app or on the website.** Say "habits that help your cycles, skin and energy".
  Treatment claims push the app towards medical-device territory. Check outcome claims in marketing with the lawyer.
- **Safety first for exercise:**
  - Pregnancy routines need a doctor's OK in the app and a list of warning signs that mean stop.
  - Every routine has "stop if pain, dizziness, bleeding or breathlessness".
- **Doctor review:** all new routines and food content is new health wording, so it needs doctor review before it ships (your
  rule). That includes an MBBS or physiotherapist for the pregnancy and after-birth moves. Write the copy in Dr. Megha's voice.
- **Shared phones:** habit words like "walk" and "dal at dinner" are safe to show. Plans follow discreet mode like everything
  else.
- **Privacy:** plans and trackers stay on her phone. Nothing goes to a server except plan progress in the doctor summary, and
  only when she ticks it.
  - It goes in as one line inside the existing "tools" section. The server only accepts its existing sections (`SHARE_KEYS`:
    about, periods, symptoms, checkins, tools, questions, notes), so this needs no server change.
  - If you want opt-in aggregate counts ("plans finished"), that's a small server change to the Version 30 counter route, and
    it needs your OK to deploy.

## 5. How to show impact (for her, and for Qura)

1. **For her:** a "Your progress" card on Home and at the end of the plan, comparing her first two weeks with her last two.
   For example: "Period pain: 7 → 4", "Active minutes: 40 → 160 a week", "Cycles: 45 → 36 days".
2. **For Qura:** optional, anonymous counts of how many started a plan, finished it and saw improvement. They only count if she
   allows it, and nothing personal is sent.
3. **For evidence:** a 12-week pilot with Qura's doctors, measuring cycle regularity, pain and PMS scores, active minutes and
   waist before and after. To publish, it needs an ethics committee and consent. That's worth doing if Qura wants the credibility.

### 8.1 My plate: local and universal food

- **Her region comes from her pincode,** with nothing sent anywhere:
  - The pincode is already on the phone, in her account details.
  - The first digits give her state (postal circles), and Care near me's offline data maps every pincode to a district and
    state.
  - The state maps to one of NIN's six regions (North, South, East, West, Central, Northeast).
  - If she has no pincode, she picks her state once.
- **Universal list:** ICMR-NIN 2024's food groups and plate are always there. Cereals and millets are at most 45% of
  calories; pulses, beans and meat up to 15%; the rest is vegetables, fruit, nuts and milk. Salt at most 5 g a day.
- **Local list:** each region's own grains, dals, greens, fruits and drinks, for example:
  - Rajasthan: bajra, jowar, moth, methi, chaas;
  - Tamil Nadu: ragi, idli and dosa, sambar dal, keerai;
  - Bengal: rice, masoor, shaak, fish;
  - the Northeast: red rice, local greens, fermented foods.

  Later: what's in season this month.
- **Her choices:** vegetarian, eggs, or non-vegetarian; and her budget (low-cost options first).
- **Three colours, not a "never eat" list:**
  - More often (green): whole grains and millets, dal and chana, vegetables and greens, curd and chaas, fruit, nuts and seeds.
  - Less often (amber): maida, sugar and mithai, cold drinks and packaged juice, fried namkeen, ultra-processed snacks, extra
    salt.
  - Avoid (red), only where the evidence is clear. In pregnancy: alcohol, raw or undercooked meat and eggs, unboiled or
    unpasteurised milk, high-mercury fish, too much caffeine. For everyone: vanaspati and other trans fats.
- **By condition:**
  - PMOS: the plate plus protein at each meal, fewer refined carbs and sweet drinks. The guideline backs no single diet.
  - Anaemia: iron foods with vitamin C, tea away from meals.
  - Pregnancy: iron, calcium and folate foods, and the GDM plate if she's diagnosed.
  - Breastfeeding: extra food and water.
  - 40+: calcium foods (ragi, til, curd, milk) and protein.
- **Folk bans and Ayurvedic rules** (for example "no papaya in pregnancy", "no curd at night") go in only if a doctor approves
  them, and labelled as tradition. Sakhi's lists stay evidence-based. Qura's Ayurvedic advice belongs in Qura's consult.
- **Data and licences:**
  - Food names and groups are free to use.
  - IFCT 2017 (NIN) gives nutrient facts such as "ragi is high in calcium", to be written in our own words with credit. Ask NIN
    before reusing its tables commercially.
  - "What India Eats" (NIN) shows regional eating patterns.

### 8.2 My body: BMI and waist

- **BMI uses the Indian cut-offs** (Misra 2009 consensus): below 18.5, 18.5 to 22.9, 23 to 24.9 (higher than ideal), and 25 or
  more. The waist cut-off is 80 cm or more. The 2025 CMC Vellore revision adds waist-to-height ratio.
- **It's optional, and she picks her focus:** "habits first" or "weight too", as the PCOS guideline's weight-inclusive option
  requires (3.6.4).
  - No "obese" label on screen. The words are "higher than ideal for Indian women".
  - The diary is 18+ only, so there's no BMI for girls.
- **Pregnancy:**
  - Her BMI before pregnancy gives the commonly used weight-gain range (IOM 2009: 11.5 to 16 kg at a normal BMI). It's shown
    only after a doctor's review, labelled "your doctor may set a different target".
  - ICMR has started a national study to set India-specific ranges.
- **What BMI changes** is the starting intensity and the impact (low-impact first at a higher BMI or with knee pain), not her
  goal.

### 8.3 This week's moves: yoga and exercise, how long

- **Inputs:**
  - her stage and condition;
  - her BMI band;
  - how active she is now (3 choices);
  - any limits: knee or back pain, pregnancy trimester, weeks since the birth.
- **What she gets:** a weekly plan built to the population targets:
  - active minutes rise to 150 a week (250 if she picked "weight too");
  - 2 strength days;
  - one yoga session a day, taking 10 to 20 minutes.
- **Yoga:** four sequences, each at 3 levels:
  - Gentle start;
  - Cycle care: baddha konasana, bhujangasana, setu bandha, cat-cow, supta baddha konasana;
  - Strength and sun salutation (surya namaskar, slow);
  - Calm (anulom vilom, bhramari).

  Poses come from the AYUSH Common Yoga Protocol, using its names and our own line drawings. Its booklet text and images aren't
  copied.
- **Evidence, said honestly:**
  - PCOS: two Indian trials in 90 girls with PCOS (Nidhi 2012). An hour of yoga a day for 12 weeks improved insulin, glucose,
    lipids and anxiety more than ordinary exercise did; BMI and waist didn't differ.
  - Period pain: yoga reviews are of moderate quality (Kim 2019 meta-analysis).
  - So the app says "helps", never "cures".
- **Pregnancy and after birth:**
  - Only pregnancy-safe moves, after her doctor's OK: no lying flat on the back after the first trimester, no deep twists or
    inversions, no holding the breath.
  - After birth: pelvic floor first, then a gradual return after the 6-week check.
  - AYUSH's separate pregnancy booklet was criticised as unreferenced, so it isn't used.
- **Every routine:** a timer, steps read aloud, a drawing for each pose, and "stop if pain, dizziness, bleeding or
  breathlessness".

### 8.4 Progress: tracked, with an honest outlook

- **What she sees, from her own entries:**
  - active minutes a week (bars against the target);
  - cycle length;
  - period pain;
  - PMS days;
  - waist and weight, if she chose them.
- **"What to expect":** a timeline, for example weeks 1 to 4 for energy and mood, 4 to 12 for habits and waist, 3 to 6 months
  for cycles. Its wording and timings need a doctor's OK.
- **No weight-loss projections or promises.** There's no "lose 5 kg in 30 days". The outlook is a timeline, not a predicted
  number.

### 8.5 The doctor button

- **Where it appears:**
  - on the plan and progress screens;
  - at week 4 if nothing has moved;
  - at the 12-week review;
  - straight away for a warning sign.

  It opens "Ask Qura to call me", and her plan line goes into the doctor summary (the "tools" section). If she ticks it, her BMI
  and waist go in "about". Both sections already exist, so there's no server change.
- **Wording:** "Ask a Qura doctor about supplements or medicines". Not "speedy results", which is a promise.
- **Scope stays as the app already says it:** Qura's doctors are BAMS, and hormone medicines need an MBBS gynaecologist, so
  Qura refers.
- **Claims:** condition-specific plans with progress claims edge towards medical-device rules. Keep them framed as wellness
  guidance that follows national guidelines, and check with the lawyer before a public launch.

### 8.6 Content to make, and who reviews it

- Region lists: 6 regions, by stage and condition, plus the universal list, in English and Hindi.
- About 15 pose drawings, 4 sequences at 3 levels, and the strength moves.
- Reviewers:
  - Dr. Megha reviews food and habits;
  - an MBBS doctor or physiotherapist reviews exercise, especially pregnancy and after birth;
  - a doctor reviews the timeline.
